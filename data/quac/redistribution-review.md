# QuAC redistribution review

## Scope

The snapshot contains the complete official validation split of QuAC, the published file `val_v0.2.json` whose SHA-256 digest is `09e622916280ba04c9352acb1bc5bbe80f11a2598f6f34e934c51d9e6570f378`: 1,000 dialogues over 1,000 Wikipedia sections, 7,354 questions, every reference answer span recorded for them, and the affirmation and continuation labels that accompany each answer. For each dialogue the article title, the section title, the summary of the article the student was shown, and the full section text the teacher read are reproduced. The training split, the hidden test split, the official scoring script, the sample prediction file, the paper, the poster, and the datasheet are not imported. Both the public repository and the website publish this content. No code or images are copied. [Validation split](https://s3.amazonaws.com/my89public/quac/val_v0.2.json) and [project page](https://quac.ai/)

## Terms

The project page links the dataset files from one sentence: "Download a copy of the dataset (distributed under the CC BY-SA 4.0 license)", with "CC BY-SA 4.0" linking the Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 4.0 International legal code. That grant permits copying, redistribution, public display, and adaptation when the work is credited, a link to the terms is given, notices are kept, changes are indicated, and adaptations are shared under the same terms. The files are served from a public Amazon S3 bucket with no access gate, no registration, and no click-through agreement; anonymous requests returned them. Submitting a model for a test-set score requires a CodaLab account, but nothing about downloading the published splits does, and this snapshot uses only a published split. [Project page](https://quac.ai/) and [legal code](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/legalcode)

The authors' datasheet states different terms: "QuAC is distributed under the CC-BY-4.0 license (corrected in Oct 2023 from MIT license)". The project page's share-alike statement and its link to the share-alike legal code are the more restrictive of the two, and they are the statement that sits beside the download links, so this snapshot is published on those terms and carries the share-alike notice below. Either statement permits this redistribution; the difference only affects what downstream reusers owe. The datasheet also asks that users cite the dataset paper, which the notices below do. [Datasheet](https://quac.ai/updated_datasheet.pdf)

The section text and article summaries are English Wikipedia prose, so they carry Wikipedia's own terms as well. Wikipedia text is shared under CC BY-SA, and Wikipedia's reuse guidance accepts a link to the article as credit to its contributors; adaptations of earlier CC BY-SA versions may be published under version 4.0, so sharing this text under CC BY-SA 4.0 is consistent with those terms, and it is a further reason to treat the project page's share-alike statement as the operative one. QuAC records the article title and section title for every dialogue but not the revision the text was taken from, so the attribution can only point at the article rather than at the text as it stood in 2018: the generated `ATTRIBUTION.md` lists all 721 articles with links, and every task prompt names its article and section. Of those 721 titles, 719 resolve on Wikipedia today and 23 of them now redirect to a renamed article. Two titles are mangled upstream by an escaping defect and do not resolve as recorded, `Billy \Crash\" Craddock"` and `\Weird Al\" Yankovic"`; they stand for the articles "Billy \"Crash\" Craddock" and "\"Weird Al\" Yankovic", which is what `ATTRIBUTION.md` links. The questions, answer spans, and dialog-act labels were produced by Amazon Mechanical Turk workers hired by the authors. [Wikipedia's reuse terms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Reusing_Wikipedia_content) and [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.07036)

A scan of the imported text found no contact details, identifiers, or other personal data beyond what Wikipedia records about public figures: the articles are all about notable people, so dates of birth, family members, relationships, illnesses, and deaths appear throughout, as they do in the encyclopedia. The datasheet states that the dialogues contain no personal information about the crowdworkers and that the articles were already public. The project page gives four authors' email addresses; they are not reproduced here.

## Changes

Benchopedia assembles each task prompt from the question, the earlier turns of its dialogue, lines naming the article and section, the article summary, and the section text. The dialogue history is written as `Student:` and `Teacher:` lines, using the question and the answer span the original teacher gave during collection, which is the history the student had seen when asking. Article titles, section titles, summaries, section text, questions, and answer spans are unchanged, including the two mangled titles above and the section titles that upstream scraped with a trailing "Edit" from the Wikipedia interface. The upstream `CANNOTANSWER` marker that ends every section text is kept.

The reference solution lists the recorded answer spans one per line, in upstream order, including the repeats that occur when annotators chose the same span. Where every recorded answer is `CANNOTANSWER`, a single `CANNOTANSWER` line is written instead of one per annotator, which is what the official scoring script does with such a question; no question in this split mixes `CANNOTANSWER` with spans, so nothing is lost. The affirmation and continuation labels follow after a blank line, written out as the paper names them rather than as the single letters the file stores. Keys are the upstream question identifiers, kept as recorded, including the one dialogue whose question numbers skip a number. Prompt and solution links point at the published split file, which has no per-question addresses, and the environment link is omitted.
